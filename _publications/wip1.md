Carbon dividends can make carbon pricing progressive, yet public support often remains weak. Douenne and Fabre (2022) link low support in a French survey to pessimistic beliefs about the policy’s personal and distributional effects. We examine whether a similar belief pattern appears in South Korea, a country with no carbon tax or history of fuel-tax protests. We combine a microsimulation based on the National Survey of Tax and Benefit with an original survey of 1,397 Korean adults. The policy scenario closely matches the French benchmark: a carbon tax of KRW 60,000/tCO2 financing an annual per-capita dividend of approximately KRW 110,000. The simulation indicates that 77.6% of households would gain. Yet only 23.6% of respondents expect to gain, and their mean expected impact is KRW −91,000, compared with a simulated mean household gain of KRW 89,000. Most respondents also fail to recognize the policy’s progressivity. Respondents who received the information statements reported only modestly different beliefs about progressivity and environmental effectiveness, echoing the limited updating found in the French study. Compared with respondents in the French benchmark study, however, Korean respondents express substantially greater support: 40.1% support the policy and 36.6% oppose it, compared with 10% and 70%, respectively, in the French study. Moreover, 11.9% support the policy despite expecting a loss, and majority non-opposition persists at perceived annual losses of about KRW 200,000. The comparison therefore documents similar incidence pessimism but markedly different patterns of policy support across the two studies.
